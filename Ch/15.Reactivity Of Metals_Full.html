Reactivity of Metals

We classify metals by their reactivity to predict their chemical behavior. This chapter covers the reactivity series, displacement reactions, and the thermal decomposition of metal compounds.

1. The Reactivity Series

Metals are arranged based on the vigor of their reactions with water, steam, and dilute acid. A more reactive metal has a greater tendency to form positive ions.

MetalSymbolReaction with WaterReaction with Dilute Acid
PotassiumKViolent, Lilac flameExplosive
SodiumNaVery rapid, FizzesExplosive
CalciumCaSteady, Grey granules sink/riseViolent
MagnesiumMgVery slow (Hot water) / Fast with steamRapid Fizzing
AluminumAlNo reaction (Oxide layer)Slow (until oxide removed)
ZincZnReacts only with steamSteady Fizzing
IronFeReacts only with steam (Reversible)Slow Fizzing
LeadPbNo reactionVery slow
CopperCuNo reactionNo reaction
Silver/GoldAg/AuNo reactionNo reaction
Placement of Carbon and Hydrogen:
Carbon is placed between Aluminum and Zinc. Metals below Carbon (Zn, Fe, Pb, Cu) can be extracted by reduction with carbon.
Hydrogen is placed between Lead and Copper. Metals above Hydrogen react with acids to produce $H_2$. Metals below Hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au) do not react with acids.

2. Displacement Reactions

A more reactive metal will displace a less reactive metal from a compound (oxide or aqueous solution). These are redox reactions.

A. In Aqueous Solution

Example: Magnesium + Copper(II) Sulfate

$Mg_{(s)} + CuSO_{4(aq)} \rightarrow MgSO_{4(aq)} + Cu_{(s)}$

  • Observation: Blue solution fades to colorless. Red-brown copper solid deposits.
  • Ionic Equation: $Mg + Cu^{2+} \rightarrow Mg^{2+} + Cu$.
  • Explanation: Mg is more reactive, so it loses electrons (oxidized) more easily than Cu.

B. With Metal Oxides (Thermite Reaction)

Example: Aluminum + Iron(III) Oxide

$2Al_{(s)} + Fe_2O_3_{(s)} \rightarrow Al_2O_3_{(s)} + 2Fe_{(l)}$

  • Highly exothermic (releases heat).
  • Produces molten iron.
  • Used to weld railway tracks.

3. Thermal Decomposition

The stability of metal compounds depends on the reactivity of the metal. More reactive metals form more stable compounds, meaning they are harder to decompose by heat.

A. Metal Carbonates

General equation: $Metal\ Carbonate \xrightarrow{heat} Metal\ Oxide + Carbon\ Dioxide$

  • Group I (Na, K): Very stable. Do not decompose with a Bunsen burner.
  • Group II & Transition (Ca, Cu): Decompose easily.
    $CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2$.
    $CuCO_3$ (Green) $\rightarrow CuO$ (Black) $+ CO_2$.

B. Metal Nitrates

General equation for most metals: $Metal\ Nitrate \rightarrow Metal\ Oxide + NO_2 + O_2$

  • Group I (Except Li): Decompose to Nitrite and Oxygen only.
    $2KNO_3 \rightarrow 2KNO_2 + O_2$. (No brown gas).
  • Group II & Transition (Mg, Cu): Decompose to Oxide, Nitrogen Dioxide ($NO_2$, Brown fumes), and Oxygen.
    $2Cu(NO_3)_2 \rightarrow 2CuO + 4NO_2 + O_2$.

C. Metal Hydroxides

  • Group I (Na, K): Stable. No decomposition.
  • Others (Ca, Cu): Decompose to Oxide + Water.
    $Cu(OH)_2 \rightarrow CuO + H_2O$.
Exam Tip: Remember the color changes in decomposition.
Copper(II) Carbonate is Green $\rightarrow$ Copper(II) Oxide is Black.
Nitrogen Dioxide ($NO_2$) is a Brown gas.
