Preparation of Salts

The method used to prepare a specific salt depends entirely on two factors: the solubility of the salt being made, and the solubility of the reactants available. This chapter requires precise memorization of solubility rules.

1. Solubility Rules (Crucial)

You cannot choose a preparation method without knowing these rules by heart.

Type of Salt Solubility Rule Exceptions (Insoluble)
Nitrates ALL Soluble None
Chlorides Most Soluble Silver (AgCl), Lead(II) (PbCl₂)
Sulfates Most Soluble Barium (BaSO₄), Calcium (CaSO₄), Lead(II) (PbSO₄)
Carbonates Most Insoluble Sodium (Na), Potassium (K), Ammonium (NH₄)
Hydroxides Most Insoluble Sodium (Na), Potassium (K), Ammonium (NH₄), Calcium (partially)
Group 1 & NH₄⁺ Salts ALL Soluble None

2. The Decision Process

Before starting, ask: Is the salt I want to make Soluble or Insoluble?

  • If Insoluble: Use Method C (Precipitation).
  • If Soluble: Ask, is the metal/base typically Group 1 (Sodium, Potassium) or Ammonium?
    • Yes (Soluble Base): Use Method B (Titration).
    • No (Insoluble Base): Use Method A (Excess Method).

Method A: Soluble Salt from Insoluble Base

(The "Excess" Method)

Used for preparing salts of metals like Magnesium, Zinc, Copper, Iron. The reactant is usually a Metal Oxide, Hydroxide, or Carbonate (which is insoluble).

Example: Preparation of Copper(II) Sulfate crystals from Copper(II) Oxide and Sulfuric Acid.

1 Warm the dilute acid in a beaker.
Reason: To increase the rate of reaction.
2 Add the insoluble base (solid) bit by bit, stirring constantly.
3 Continue adding until the solid is in EXCESS (stops disappearing).
Reason: To ensure all the acid has reacted. Acid is dangerous to leave in the solution.
4 Filter the mixture.
Reason: To remove the excess unreacted solid base. The filtrate is the salt solution.
5 Transfer filtrate to an evaporating basin. Heat to evaporate some water until the point of crystallization is reached.
Reason: We do NOT heat to dryness because some salts decompose, and we need water of crystallization for hydrated crystals.
6 Allow the solution to cool. Crystals will form. Filter the crystals, wash with a little distilled water, and dry between filter papers.
Exam Trap: Never say "Evaporate to dryness" if the question asks for crystals. Evaporating to dryness produces an anhydrous powder. Cooling a saturated solution produces hydrated crystals.

Method B: Soluble Salt from Soluble Base

(The "Titration" Method)

Used for Group 1 Metals (Na, K) and Ammonium salts. Since both reactants are liquids (or soluble), we cannot just "add excess" because we wouldn't be able to filter it out.

Example: Preparation of Sodium Chloride from NaOH and HCl.

1 Use a pipette to measure a fixed volume (e.g., 25.0 cm³) of alkali into a conical flask. Add a few drops of indicator (e.g., Phenolphthalein).
2 Fill a burette with acid. Add acid to the alkali dropwise until the indicator changes color (End Point).
3 Record the exact volume of acid needed to neutralize the alkali.
4 Repeat the experiment WITHOUT the indicator, using the exact same volumes.
Reason: The indicator is an impurity. We want pure salt crystals.
5 Evaporate water from the resulting neutral solution to obtain crystals (Crystallization).

Method C: Insoluble Salt from Two Soluble Salts

(The "Precipitation" Method)

Used to prepare insoluble salts (e.g., Barium Sulfate, Silver Chloride, Lead Iodide). You must mix two soluble reagents containing the ions you need.

Example: Preparation of Barium Sulfate (BaSO₄).
Reactants: Barium Nitrate (Soluble) + Sodium Sulfate (Soluble).

1 Mix the two solutions in a beaker. A solid precipitate forms immediately.
2 Filter the mixture.
Reason: The residue (solid trapped in paper) is the salt we want.
3 Wash the residue with distilled water.
Reason: To wash away soluble impurities (e.g., Sodium Nitrate solution sticking to the solid).
4 Dry the residue in a warm oven or between filter papers.
Ionic Equations for Precipitation
The exams often ask for the ionic equation. Ignore the "Spectator Ions" (the ones that stay in solution).

Full Eq: Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaNO₃(aq)
Ionic Eq: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

3. Hydrated vs. Anhydrous Salts

Many salts prepared by crystallization contain water molecules trapped inside the lattice. This is called Water of Crystallization.

  • Hydrated: Contains water (e.g., CuSO₄•5H₂O, Blue crystals).
  • Anhydrous: No water (e.g., CuSO₄, White powder).

Heating hydrated salts removes the water (reversible reaction):
CuSO₄•5H₂O (s) ⇌ CuSO₄ (s) + 5H₂O (l) (Blue → White)
