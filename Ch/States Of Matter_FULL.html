States of Matter

This topic covers the fundamental properties of solids, liquids, and gases, the Kinetic Particle Theory describing their structure, and the thermodynamics of state changes and diffusion.

1. The Three States of Matter

Matter is defined as anything that has mass and takes up space (volume). The three states are distinguished by their physical properties, which are determined by the arrangement of their particles.

2. Kinetic Particle Theory

The Kinetic Particle Theory states that all matter is made up of tiny particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) that are in constant motion. The state of matter depends on the forces of attraction and the kinetic energy of the particles.

Structure Definitions (Memorize keywords):
  • Solids: Particles are packed closely in a regular lattice. They vibrate about fixed positions. Strong forces of attraction.
  • Liquids: Particles are close together but arranged randomly. They can slide over each other. Forces are weaker than in solids.
  • Gases: Particles are far apart and arranged randomly. They move randomly at high speeds. Negligible forces of attraction.

3. Changes of State

Changes of state occur when energy is absorbed (endothermic) or released (exothermic). Importantly, during a change of state, the temperature remains constant.

  • Melting: Solid to Liquid (Heat absorbed).
  • Boiling: Liquid to Gas (Heat absorbed at fixed temp).
  • Freezing: Liquid to Solid (Heat released).
  • Condensation: Gas to Liquid (Heat released).
  • Sublimation: Solid to Gas directly (e.g., Dry Ice, Iodine).
  • Deposition: Gas to Solid directly.
Exam Tip: Boiling vs. Evaporation
Boiling occurs at a specific temperature (boiling point) and happens throughout the liquid (bubbles form). Evaporation occurs at any temperature and happens only at the surface.

Heating Curves

When a solid is heated, its particles vibrate faster. At the melting point, the temperature stops rising (a plateau on the graph). This is because the heat energy is used to break the attractive bonds holding the lattice together, rather than increasing kinetic energy (temperature).

4. Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement.

Factors Affecting Diffusion Rate

  1. Temperature: Higher temperature means particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, increasing the rate of diffusion.
  2. Molecular Mass ($M_r$): Lighter particles diffuse faster than heavier particles.
Classic Experiment: HCl vs Ammonia ($NH_3$)
When cotton wool soaked in Conc. HCl ($M_r = 36.5$) and Conc. Ammonia ($M_r = 17$) are placed at opposite ends of a glass tube, a white ring of Ammonium Chloride forms.

Result: The ring forms closer to the HCl end.
Explanation: Ammonia particles are lighter than HCl particles, so they diffuse faster and travel further in the same time.

5. Brownian Motion

This is the random, erratic movement of microscopic particles (like pollen grains or smoke) suspended in a fluid (liquid or gas). It is caused by the bombardment of the visible particles by the invisible, fast-moving fluid molecules. This provides evidence for the kinetic particle theory.

6. Gas Pressure

Pressure in a gas is caused by the particles colliding with the walls of the container. When they hit the wall, they exert a force. Higher temperature increases pressure because particles hit the walls harder and more frequently.
