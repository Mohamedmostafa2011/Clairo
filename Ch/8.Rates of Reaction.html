Rates of Reaction

This chapter examines how fast chemical reactions occur, the factors that influence this speed (Collision Theory), how we measure it, and the concept of photochemical reactions.

1. Collision Theory

For a chemical reaction to occur, particles must collide. However, not all collisions result in a reaction. For a collision to be successful (effective), two conditions must be met:

Conditions for a Successful Collision:
  1. Correct Orientation: Particles must collide at the right angle.
  2. Activation Energy ($E_a$): Particles must collide with energy equal to or greater than the minimum activation energy required to break existing bonds.

2. Factors Affecting Rate

The rate of reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. We can increase the rate by increasing the frequency of successful collisions.

FactorEffect on RateExplanation (Collision Theory)
ConcentrationIncreasesMore particles per unit volume leading to more frequent collisions.
Pressure (Gases)IncreasesParticles are squeezed closer together (same as concentration), causing more frequent collisions.
Surface Area (Solids)IncreasesMore particles are exposed on the surface, available for collision. (Powder > Lumps).
TemperatureIncreases significantlyParticles gain kinetic energy and move faster (more collisions). Crucially, more particles have energy $\ge E_a$, so a higher proportion of collisions are successful.
CatalystIncreasesProvides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower Activation Energy.

3. Measuring Rates of Reaction

We measure rate by observing how quickly a reactant is used up or a product is formed.

A. Collecting Gas (Volume vs Time)

Used when a gas is produced (e.g., Mg + HCl $\rightarrow$ $H_2$). A gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder is used.

  • The gradient of the Volume vs. Time graph represents the rate.
  • Steepest at the start (highest concentration).
  • Becomes horizontal when the reaction stops (reactant used up).

B. Mass Loss (Mass vs Time)

Used when a heavy gas (like $CO_2$) escapes. The flask is placed on a balance. Cotton wool is placed in the neck to let gas escape but prevent liquid spitting.

C. Disappearing Cross (Precipitate)

Used for reactions that produce a solid precipitate (e.g., Sodium Thiosulfate + HCl $\rightarrow$ Sulfur). Time taken for a cross under the flask to disappear is measured.

Exam Tip: Interpreting Graphs
A steeper curve means a faster rate. If two curves level off at the same height, the same amount of product was produced (same limiting reactant). If one curve levels off lower, less reactant was used.

4. Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction but remains chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.

  • It lowers the Activation Energy ($E_a$).
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts.
  • Transition metals (e.g., Iron in Haber Process) are common industrial catalysts.

5. Dangers of Explosive Combustion

Extremely fast reactions where gas is produced and heat is released rapidly can cause explosions. This is a risk when fine powders or gases mix with air.

  • Flour Mills: Flour dust has a massive surface area. A spark can trigger a massive explosion.
  • Coal Mines: Methane gas or coal dust mixed with air can explode.

6. Photochemical Reactions

These are reactions initiated or accelerated by light. The rate increases as light intensity increases.

Examples:

  1. Photosynthesis: Plants use light energy to convert $CO_2$ and water into glucose. Chlorophyll is the catalyst.
    $6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2$
  2. Silver Salts (Photography): Silver halides (e.g., AgBr) decompose in light to form silver metal (dark grey). This is the basis of film photography.
    $2AgBr \rightarrow 2Ag + Br_2$
