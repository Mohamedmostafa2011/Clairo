Kinetic Particle Theory

Memorise These Definitions

  • Solids: Particles packed closely in a regular lattice. Vibrate about fixed positions. Strong forces.
  • Liquids: Particles close together but arranged randomly. Slide over each other. Weaker forces.
  • Gases: Particles far apart, arranged randomly. Move randomly at high speeds. Negligible forces.

Changes of State

MeltingSolid → Liquid (heat absorbed — endothermic)
BoilingLiquid → Gas at fixed temp (heat absorbed)
FreezingLiquid → Solid (heat released — exothermic)
CondensationGas → Liquid (heat released)
SublimationSolid → Gas directly (e.g., dry ice, iodine)
DepositionGas → Solid directly

⚡ Exam Tip — Boiling vs Evaporation

  • Boiling: At a specific temperature; happens throughout the liquid (bubbles form).
  • Evaporation: At any temperature; happens only at the surface.

Diffusion

  • Net movement from high → low concentration down a gradient.
  • Faster at higher temperature; lighter molecules diffuse faster.

Key Definitions

Must Memorise

  • Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons in nucleus = number of electrons in neutral atom.
  • Mass Number (A): Total protons + neutrons in nucleus.
  • Neutrons: A − Z
  • Isotopes: Same element (same Z), different number of neutrons (different A).

Electron Shells

Shell 1Max 2 electrons
Shell 2Max 8 electrons
Shell 3Max 8 electrons (at IGCSE level)
Noble Gas config.Full outer shell = chemically stable (unreactive)

RAM & Relative Atomic Mass

  • Relative Atomic Mass (Ar): Average mass of naturally occurring atoms compared to 1/12 of Carbon-12.
  • Chlorine isotopes: Cl-35 (75%) and Cl-37 (25%) → Ar = (75×35 + 25×37)/100 = 35.5

Chapter 03

Chemical Bonding

Ionic Bonding

  • Between Metal + Non-Metal
  • Metal loses electrons → cation (+)
  • Non-metal gains electrons → anion (−)
  • Strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
  • Forms giant lattice structure

Covalent Bonding

  • Between Non-Metal + Non-Metal
  • Atoms share electrons
  • Single bond = 1 shared pair
  • Double bond = 2 shared pairs
  • Forms molecules or giant covalent structures

Properties of Ionic Compounds

PropertyObservationExplanation
Melting PointHighStrong electrostatic forces require much energy to break
VolatilityNon-volatileStrong bonds — do not evaporate easily
Conductivity (solid)Does NOT conductIons fixed in lattice, cannot move
Conductivity (liquid/aq)ConductsIons free to move and carry charge

Properties of Simple Molecular Covalent

PropertyExplanation
Low melting/boiling pointWeak intermolecular forces between molecules (not the strong covalent bonds)
Usually gas/liquid at RTPEasy to overcome weak forces
Do NOT conduct electricityNo free ions or electrons

Giant Covalent (e.g. Diamond, Silicon Dioxide)

  • Diamond: Each C bonded to 4 others. Very hard, very high melting point, does not conduct.
  • Graphite: Layers of hexagonal C. Each C bonded to 3. Delocalised electrons → conducts electricity. Layers slide → lubricant.
  • SiO₂: Very high melting point, does not conduct.

Metallic Bonding

  • Regular lattice of positive ions surrounded by a "sea" of delocalised electrons.
  • Conducts electricity: delocalised electrons carry charge.
  • Malleable/ductile: layers slide without breaking bond.

Chapter 04

Formulae, Equations & Stoichiometry

Balancing Equations — Steps

  • Write correct formulae for every reactant and product.
  • Count atoms of each element on both sides.
  • Add big numbers (coefficients) in front of formulae. NEVER change subscripts!

The Mole Concept

1 Mole6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number)
Molar MassMass of 1 mole in grams = numerically equal to Ar or Mr
n = m/Mmoles = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol)
Concentrationmol/dm³ = moles ÷ volume (dm³)
Gas Volume1 mole of any gas at RTP = 24 dm³ (24,000 cm³)

Key Formulae

Molesn = m ÷ M
Concentrationc = n ÷ V
Gas VolumeV = n × 24 dm³ (at RTP)
% Yield(actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
% Purity(mass of pure substance ÷ total mass) × 100

Empirical vs Molecular Formula

  • Empirical formula: Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms (e.g., CH₂O).
  • Molecular formula: Actual number of atoms in a molecule (e.g., C₆H₁₂O₆).
  • To find empirical formula: divide % by Ar, then find simplest ratio.

Chapter 05

Chemical Energetics

Exothermic vs Endothermic

FeatureExothermic 🔥Endothermic ❄️
Heat transferReleased to surroundingsAbsorbed from surroundings
TemperatureSurroundings temperature risesSurroundings temperature falls
Enthalpy ΔHNegative (−ve)Positive (+ve)
Energy contentProducts have less energyProducts have more energy
ExamplesCombustion, neutralisation, respirationThermal decomposition, photosynthesis

Bond Energy Calculations

  • Energy input = breaking bonds (endothermic)
  • Energy output = forming bonds (exothermic)
  • ΔH = Energy to break bonds − Energy released forming bonds
  • If ΔH is negative → exothermic reaction overall.

Activation Energy (Ea)

Activation EnergyMinimum energy particles need to collide successfully and start a reaction. Shown as the "hump" on an energy level diagram.
CatalystLowers activation energy by providing an alternative reaction pathway. Not consumed in the reaction.

Heating Curves

  • During a change of state, temperature remains constant.
  • Heat energy is used to break attractive bonds, not increase kinetic energy.
  • Flat sections on graph = melting point or boiling point.

Electrochemistry

Key Definitions

ElectrolysisBreakdown of an ionic compound (molten or in solution) by passage of electricity.
ElectrolyteCompound that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved (free-moving ions).
ElectrodeRod (usually graphite or platinum) that carries current into the liquid.
PANIC
Positive Anode Negative Is Cathode

Electron Flow

  • Anode (+): Anions (−) attracted. Lose electrons → Oxidation.
  • Cathode (−): Cations (+) attracted. Gain electrons → Reduction.

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

ElectrolyteAt Cathode (−)At Anode (+)
Dilute H₂SO₄ / NaOHHydrogen (H₂)Oxygen (O₂)
Dilute NaCl (aq)Hydrogen (H₂)Oxygen (O₂)
Concentrated NaCl (aq)Hydrogen (H₂)Chlorine (Cl₂)
CuSO₄ (aq)Copper (Cu)Oxygen (O₂)

Discharge Priority at Cathode

  • Metal ions discharged in preference to H⁺ if the metal is below hydrogen in the reactivity series.
  • At anode: Cl⁻ or Br⁻ discharged in preference to OH⁻ only in concentrated solution.

Chapter 07

Rates of Reaction

Collision Theory — Successful Collision Conditions

  • Correct orientation: Particles must collide at the right angle.
  • Activation Energy: Particles must have energy ≥ Ea to break existing bonds.

Factors Affecting Rate

FactorEffect on RateExplanation (Collision Theory)
Concentration (aq)IncreasesMore particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions.
Pressure (gases)IncreasesParticles squeezed closer together → more frequent collisions.
Surface Area (solids)IncreasesMore particles exposed on surface. Powder > Lumps.
TemperatureIncreases significantlyParticles have more KE, move faster, AND more particles have energy ≥ Ea.
CatalystIncreasesLowers Ea → greater proportion of particles have sufficient energy.

Measuring Rate of Reaction

  • Gas collected: measure volume vs time
  • Precipitate formed: time for cross to disappear (turbidity)
  • Mass change: measure mass lost on balance (gas escaping)
  • Colour change: colorimetry

Chapter 08

Reversible Reactions & Equilibrium

Dynamic Equilibrium

Key Characteristics

  • Occurs in a closed system.
  • Forward and reverse reactions occur simultaneously.
  • Rates of forward and reverse reactions are equal.
  • Concentrations of reactants and products remain constant (not necessarily equal).

Le Chatelier's Principle

The Principle

  • If a change is made to a system at equilibrium, the system shifts to oppose that change.
ChangeShift Direction
Increase concentration of reactant→ Forward (more products)
Increase concentration of product← Backward (more reactants)
Increase pressure→ Side with fewer moles of gas
Decrease pressure→ Side with more moles of gas
Increase temperature→ Endothermic direction
Decrease temperature→ Exothermic direction
Add catalystNo shift; reaches equilibrium faster

Haber Process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃)

Industrial Conditions & Reasons

  • Temperature: ~450°C — Compromise. Low T gives more yield (exothermic) but too slow. 450°C balances yield and rate.
  • Pressure: ~200 atm — High pressure favours products (4 moles gas → 2 moles). Higher pressure = more cost.
  • Catalyst: Iron — Lowers Ea, increases rate without shifting equilibrium.

Tests for Water Presence

Cobalt(II) ChlorideBlue (anhydrous) → Pink (hydrated) — presence of water
Copper(II) SulfateWhite (anhydrous) → Blue (hydrated) — presence of water

Chapter 09

Redox Reactions

OIL RIG
Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons)  |  Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

Three Ways to Define Redox

DefinitionOxidationReduction
1. OxygenGain of oxygenLoss of oxygen
2. Electrons (most important)Loss of electronsGain of electrons
3. Oxidation NumberIncrease in numberDecrease in number

Oxidising Agent

  • Causes oxidation of another substance
  • Is itself reduced (gains electrons)
  • Examples: O₂, Cl₂, H₂O₂, KMnO₄

Reducing Agent

  • Causes reduction of another substance
  • Is itself oxidised (loses electrons)
  • Examples: H₂, C, CO, metals

Oxidation Number Rules

Free elementAlways 0 (e.g., O₂, Fe, Na)
Monatomic ionEquals the ion's charge (e.g., Na⁺ = +1)
OxygenUsually −2 (except in peroxides: −1; F₂O: +2)
HydrogenUsually +1 (except metal hydrides: −1)

Chapter 10

Acids and Bases

Precise Definitions

AcidProton donor. Dissolves in water to produce H⁺ ions. HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
BaseProton acceptor. Usually a metal oxide or metal hydroxide.
AlkaliA soluble base. Produces OH⁻ ions in water. NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Strong vs Weak Acids

Strong Acid

  • Completely ionises in water
  • Examples: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃
  • Lower pH for same concentration

Weak Acid

  • Partially ionises in water (equilibrium)
  • Examples: CH₃COOH (ethanoic acid), H₂CO₃
  • Higher pH for same concentration

MASH Reactions (Acids + ...)

MetalMetal + Acid → Salt + Hydrogen
Metal OxideMetal Oxide + Acid → Salt + Water
Metal HydroxideMetal Hydroxide + Acid → Salt + Water
CarbonateMetal Carbonate + Acid → Salt + Water + CO₂
AmmoniaAmmonia + Acid → Ammonium Salt

The pH Scale

  • pH 1–6: Acid (lower = stronger)
  • pH 7: Neutral
  • pH 8–14: Alkali (higher = stronger)
  • Universal indicator: Red (acid) → Green (neutral) → Purple (alkali)

Preparation of Salts

Solubility Rules — Must Memorise!

Type of SaltSolubility RuleExceptions (Insoluble)
NitratesALL SolubleNone
ChloridesMost SolubleAgCl, PbCl₂
SulfatesMost SolubleBaSO₄, CaSO₄, PbSO₄
CarbonatesMost InsolubleSodium, Potassium, Ammonium
HydroxidesMost InsolubleNaOH, KOH, NH₄OH, Ca(OH)₂ (partial)
Group 1 + NH₄⁺ saltsALL SolubleNone

Decision Process

1
Is the salt soluble or insoluble? (Check solubility rules above)
2
If insoluble → Use Method C: Precipitation (mix two aqueous solutions)
3
If soluble → Is the metal/base Group 1 (Na, K) or NH₄⁺?
4
If Yes (soluble base) → Use Method B: Titration
5
If No (insoluble base/metal/carbonate) → Use Method A: Excess Solid Method

Methods Summary

Method A — Excess Solid

  • Add excess insoluble solid (metal, oxide, carbonate, hydroxide) to acid.
  • Filter to remove excess solid. Evaporate solution to crystallise salt.

Method B — Titration (for Na, K, NH₄ salts)

  • Titrate alkali with acid using indicator to find exact volumes.
  • Repeat with exact volumes, no indicator. Evaporate to crystallise.

Method C — Precipitation

  • Mix two aqueous solutions whose ions form an insoluble salt.
  • Filter, wash with distilled water, dry the precipitate.

The Periodic Table

Structure of the Table

Groups (vertical)Same number of outer shell electrons → similar chemical properties.
Periods (horizontal)Same number of electron shells. Character changes from metallic → non-metallic left to right.

Group I — Alkali Metals (Li, Na, K)

PropertyTrend Going Down (Li → K)
Melting PointDecreases (Li highest)
DensityIncreases (Li lightest)
ReactivityIncreases (K most reactive)
+Water2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)
+Oxygen4M(s) + O₂(g) → 2M₂O(s)
+Chlorine2M(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2MCl(s)

Group VII — Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I)

PropertyTrend Going Down (F → I)
Melting/Boiling PointIncreases
ReactivityDecreases (F most reactive)
ColourBecomes darker (F: pale yellow → I: grey/black)

Displacement Reactions

  • A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from its salt solution.
  • Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq) ✓
  • I₂(aq) + 2KCl(aq) → No reaction ✗ (Iodine less reactive than Cl)

Transition Elements

Properties

  • Located between Groups II and III (d-block).
  • Form coloured compounds (e.g., Cu²⁺ = blue, Fe³⁺ = orange/brown).
  • Variable oxidation states (e.g., Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺).
  • Good catalysts (e.g., Fe in Haber process, V₂O₅ in Contact process).
  • High density, high melting points compared to Group I.

Metallic Elements & Alloys

Properties of Metals

ConductivityConduct heat and electricity due to a "sea" of delocalised electrons that are free to move.
MalleabilityCan be hammered into shape. Layers of positive ions slide over each other without breaking the metallic bond.
DuctilityCan be drawn into wires for same reason as malleability.
StructureRegular lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.

Alloys

Why are alloys harder than pure metals?

  • Atoms of different sizes are introduced into the lattice.
  • These disrupt the regular arrangement of layers.
  • The layers can no longer slide easily → harder and stronger.

Position of Carbon & Hydrogen

  • Carbon is placed between Aluminium and Zinc. Metals below Carbon (Zn, Fe, Pb, Cu) can be extracted by reduction with Carbon.
  • Hydrogen is placed between Lead and Copper. Metals above H react with dilute acid to produce H₂. Metals below H (Cu, Ag, Au) do not.

Displacement Reactions

Rule

  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its solution.
  • Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s) ✓ (Iron is more reactive than Copper)
  • Cu(s) + ZnSO₄(aq) → No reaction ✗ (Copper less reactive than Zinc)

Extraction of Iron — Blast Furnace

1
Combustion: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + Heat
2
Reducing agent formed: CO₂(g) + C(s) → 2CO(g)
3
Reduction of iron ore: Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g)
4
Removal of impurities: Limestone (CaCO₃) → CaO + CO₂, then CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag)

Rusting & Corrosion Prevention

Conditions for Rusting

  • Both oxygen AND water must be present. Neither alone causes rusting.
  • Equation: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 6H₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·3H₂O (hydrated iron oxide = rust)
Prevention MethodHow it works
Painting / CoatingPhysical barrier — keeps out O₂ and H₂O
Galvanising (Zinc coat)Zinc sacrifices itself (more reactive). Barrier + sacrificial protection.
Sacrificial ProtectionMore reactive metal (Mg or Zn) attached — corrodes instead of iron.
Stainless SteelAlloying with Cr and Ni — inherently corrosion-resistant.

Chapter 17

Reactions of Organic Compounds

Alkane Reactions

Combustion (main reaction)

  • Complete combustion: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (excess O₂)
  • Incomplete combustion: Produces CO (toxic) and/or C (soot) when O₂ is limited.

Substitution with Halogens (UV light required)

  • CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (UV light initiates reaction)
  • Called free radical substitution. H atoms replaced by halogen atoms.

Alkene Reactions

ReactionReagent/ConditionProduct
Addition with H₂Ni catalyst, heat (hydrogenation)Alkane
Addition with Br₂Bromine water (room temp)Dibromoalkane (decolourises)
Addition with H₂OSteam, H₃PO₄ catalyst, high tempAlcohol (hydration)
Addition with HCl/HBrRoom temperatureHalogenoalkane
PolymerisationHigh pressure, catalystAddition polymer

Alcohol Reactions

Key Reactions of Alcohols

  • Combustion: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O (clean burning fuel)
  • Oxidation → Carboxylic Acid: C₂H₅OH + [O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O (using acidified KMnO₄)
  • Dehydration → Alkene: C₂H₅OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (Al₂O₃ catalyst, heat)
  • Esterification: Alcohol + Carboxylic Acid → Ester + Water (H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat)
  • Fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ (yeast, 37°C, anaerobic)

Chapter 18

Petrochemicals & Polymers

Crude Oil & Fractional Distillation

How It Works

  • Crude oil heated and vaporised. Vapours rise up fractionating column.
  • As they rise, they cool down. Each fraction condenses at a different level.
  • Shorter chains → lower boiling point → condense at top.
  • Longer chains → higher boiling point → condense at bottom.
FractionCarbon ChainBoiling PointMain Use
Refinery GasC₁–C₄Below 25°CFuel (LPG)
Gasoline (Petrol)C₅–C₁₀25–60°CCar engines
NaphthaC₇–C₁₂60–180°CFeedstock for chemicals
KeroseneC₁₁–C₁₆150–250°CJet fuel, heating
DieselC₁₅–C₂₅220–350°CDiesel engines
Fuel OilC₂₀–C₇₀350–400°CShips, power stations
BitumenC₇₀+Above 400°CRoad surfacing, roofing

Cracking

Why Crack?

  • Long-chain alkanes are in low demand; short-chain alkenes are in high demand.
  • Thermal cracking: Very high temperature and pressure. Produces alkanes and alkenes.
  • Catalytic cracking: High temperature, zeolite catalyst. More efficient. Produces branched alkanes + alkenes.
  • Example: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₅H₁₂ + C₅H₁₀

Polymers

Addition Polymerisation

  • Alkene monomers join together; C=C opens up.
  • No other product formed.
  • e.g., ethene → poly(ethene) [polythene]
  • e.g., chloroethene → PVC
  • e.g., styrene → polystyrene

Condensation Polymerisation

  • Two different monomers with two different functional groups.
  • Small molecule (usually H₂O) released each time.
  • e.g., Nylon (diamine + dicarboxylic acid)
  • e.g., Polyester (diol + dicarboxylic acid)

Chapter 19

Chemistry of Our Environment

The Atmosphere

Composition (Mnemonic: 78-21-1 "Neon Lights Are Off")

  • ~78% Nitrogen (N₂)
  • ~21% Oxygen (O₂)
  • ~1% Argon (Ar)
  • ~0.04% Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)
  • Variable: Water vapour + traces

Air Pollution & Pollutant Gases

PollutantSourceEnvironmental Effect
Carbon Monoxide (CO)Incomplete combustion of carbon fuelsToxic — binds to haemoglobin, reduces O₂ transport
Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)Burning sulfur-containing fuels (coal, oil)Acid rain → damages buildings, kills trees, acidifies lakes
Oxides of Nitrogen (NOₓ)High temp in car engines (N₂ + O₂)Acid rain; photochemical smog
Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)Combustion of all carbon fuelsEnhanced greenhouse effect → global warming
Methane (CH₄)Livestock, landfill, wetlandsPotent greenhouse gas
CFCsOld aerosols, fridgesOzone layer depletion

The Greenhouse Effect

How It Works

  • Sun's radiation passes through atmosphere and warms Earth's surface.
  • Earth re-emits heat (infrared radiation).
  • Greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, H₂O) absorb and re-emit this heat → atmosphere warms.
  • Enhanced greenhouse effect: human activities increase CO₂ and CH₄ → excess warming.

Water Treatment

1
Sedimentation: Large particles settle by gravity.
2
Filtration: Water passed through fine sand/gravel beds to remove smaller particles.
3
Chlorination: Chlorine added to kill bacteria and microorganisms.

Acid Rain

  • SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (sulfurous acid)
  • 2SO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid)
  • Effects: Corrodes limestone buildings, kills fish in lakes, damages forests.
  • Solution: Remove sulfur before burning; use catalytic converters; use lime to neutralise lakes.
