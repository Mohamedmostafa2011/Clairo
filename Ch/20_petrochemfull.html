Petrochemicals & Polymers

This chapter covers crude oil as a source of hydrocarbons, how alkanes and alkenes are obtained and used, and how polymers are made — both addition and condensation types.

1. Crude Oil & Fractional Distillation

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons (compounds containing C and H only). It is separated into useful fractions by fractional distillation.

How Fractional Distillation Works:
Crude oil is heated and vaporised. The vapours rise up the fractionating column. As they rise, they cool down. Each fraction condenses at a different level — shorter chains condense at the top (lower boiling point), longer chains at the bottom (higher boiling point).
Fraction Carbon Chain Length Boiling Point Main Uses
Refinery GasC1–C4Lowest (below 25°C)Fuel for heating & cooking (LPG)
Gasoline (Petrol)C5–C1025–60°CFuel for car engines
NaphthaC7–C1260–180°CFeedstock for chemicals
Kerosene (Paraffin)C11–C16150–250°CJet fuel, heating
DieselC15–C25220–350°CDiesel engines, lorries
Fuel OilC20–C70350–400°CShip fuel, power stations
Bitumen (Residue)C70+Highest (above 400°C)Road surfacing, roofing
Remember the trend: As chain length increases → Boiling point increases, Viscosity increases, Flammability decreases. Shorter chains are more useful as fuels.

2. Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons — all C–C bonds are single bonds. General formula: CnH2n+2

NameFormulaStructure
MethaneCH4CH4
EthaneC2H6CH3–CH3
PropaneC3H8CH3–CH2–CH3
ButaneC4H10CH3–(CH2)2–CH3

Combustion of Alkanes

  • Complete combustion (excess O2): Produces CO2 + H2O (blue flame).
  • Incomplete combustion (limited O2): Produces CO (toxic) + C (soot) + H2O (yellow/orange flame).
CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O    (Complete)
2CH4 + 3O2 → 2CO + 4H2O    (Incomplete)
Saturated = Single bonds only. Alkane = No double bond. "Ane sounds plain."

3. Cracking

There is a high demand for short-chain alkanes (petrol) but an excess of long-chain alkanes. Cracking breaks large molecules into smaller, more useful ones.

Cracking: The breaking of long-chain alkanes into shorter alkanes + alkenes using heat (and sometimes a catalyst).

Condition: High temperature (~700°C) and/or a catalyst (aluminium oxide / silica).
Type of Reaction: Thermal decomposition.

Example of Cracking

C10H22 → C8H18 + C2H4

Decane → Octane (useful fuel) + Ethene (for making polymers)

Exam tip: Cracking always produces at least one alkene (unsaturated). You can identify this because the products will have fewer H atoms relative to C atoms (CnH2n). Balance C atoms first, then check H.

4. Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons — they contain a C=C double bond. General formula: CnH2n

NameFormulaStructure
EtheneC2H4CH2=CH2
PropeneC3H6CH2=CH–CH3
ButeneC4H8CH2=CH–CH2–CH3

Addition Reactions of Alkenes

Because alkenes have a C=C double bond, they can undergo addition reactions — molecules add across the double bond, which opens up to form a single bond.

ReactionReagentConditionProduct
HydrogenationH2Nickel catalyst, 150°CAlkane
HalogenationBr2 (bromine water)Room temperatureDibromoalkane
HydrationH2O (steam)Phosphoric acid catalyst, 300°C, 60 atmAlcohol (ethanol)
HydrohalogenationHBr / HClRoom temperatureHaloalkane

Test for Alkenes (Bromine Water Test)

Add bromine water (orange/brown) to the compound.

✅ Alkene: Bromine water is decolourised (becomes colourless) — Br2 adds across the double bond.
❌ Alkane: No change — bromine water stays orange.
CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2Br–CH2Br

5. Addition Polymerisation

Many small monomer molecules (alkenes) join together to form a very long chain called a polymer. No other product is formed — just the polymer chain.

Monomer: Small molecule with a C=C double bond.
Polymer: Very long chain formed when many monomers join.
Condition: High pressure, catalyst.

The double bond opens → each monomer forms two new single bonds to the next monomer.

Key Addition Polymers (Must Know)

MonomerPolymer NameUses
Ethene (CH2=CH2)Poly(ethene) — PolyethylenePlastic bags, bottles, pipes
Propene (CH2=CHCH3)Poly(propene) — PolypropyleneRopes, crates, food containers
Chloroethene (CH2=CHCl)Poly(chloroethene) — PVCPipes, window frames, insulation
Tetrafluoroethene (CF2=CF2)Poly(tetrafluoroethene) — PTFE (Teflon)Non-stick cookware, plumber's tape
Styrene (C6H5CH=CH2)PolystyrenePackaging foam, cups, insulation

How to Draw the Repeat Unit

In the repeat unit, the double bond is gone. Instead, single bonds go out from both ends (shown with brackets and n).

n CH2=CH2 → [–CH2–CH2–]n
Ethene monomer → Poly(ethene) repeat unit
n CH2=CHCl → [–CH2–CHCl–]n
Chloroethene monomer → PVC repeat unit
Exam tip: To find the monomer from the polymer, add a C=C double bond between every two carbons in the repeat unit. To find the polymer from the monomer, remove the double bond and add brackets with n.

6. Condensation Polymerisation

In condensation polymerisation, monomers join together and a small molecule is lost (usually water H2O, or hydrogen chloride HCl) each time two monomers join.

Key difference from addition polymerisation:
• Addition: Only one type of monomer, no by-product.
• Condensation: Usually two different monomers, small molecule (H2O) released as by-product.

Nylon (Polyamide)

Made from a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid. An amide bond (–CO–NH–) forms each time, with water lost.

–NH–(CH2)6–NH– + –CO–(CH2)4–CO– → [–NH–(CH2)6–NH–CO–(CH2)4–CO–]n + nH2O

Polyester (Terylene / PET)

Made from a diol and a dicarboxylic acid. An ester bond (–COO–) forms with water lost.

–OH + –COOH → –COO– + H2O
PolymerTypeBond FormedUses
NylonPolyamideAmide (–CONH–)Clothing, ropes, toothbrush bristles, parachutes
Terylene (PET)PolyesterEster (–COO–)Clothing, plastic bottles, fabric

8. Disposal of Plastics — Problems & Solutions

Most synthetic polymers are non-biodegradable — they do not break down naturally in the environment, causing pollution problems.

ProblemDetail
Landfill wastePlastics take hundreds of years to decompose, filling up land.
Ocean pollutionPlastics break into microplastics, harming marine life.
Toxic fumes if burnedPVC releases HCl when burned. Burning also releases CO2.

Solutions

  • Recycling: Mechanical recycling — melting and reshaping thermoplastics. Chemical recycling — cracking polymers back to monomers.
  • Biodegradable plastics: Made from plant starch — break down naturally.
  • Reduce & Reuse: Using fewer plastic products.
  • Incineration with energy recovery: Burning to generate electricity, but produces CO2.
Exam tip: Know the difference between thermoplastics (soften on heating → can be recycled, e.g. poly(ethene)) and thermosetting plastics (do NOT soften → cannot be melted and reshaped, e.g. Bakelite, melamine). Addition polymers are usually thermoplastic.
