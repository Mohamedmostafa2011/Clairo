⚡ Quick Reference Cheat Sheet

▼

Subject-Verb Agreement

  • Compound "and": plural verb
  • Or/Nor: verb matches nearest subject
  • Everyone/each/neither: singular
  • "The number of": singular
  • "A number of": plural
  • Collective nouns: usually singular

Pronoun Cases

  • Subject: I, he, she, we, they, who
  • Object: me, him, her, us, them, whom
  • Trick: Remove other nouns to test
  • Reflexive: only for emphasis/same subject
  • Its/It's: its = possessive, it's = it is

Sentence Errors

  • Run-on: two sentences without punctuation
  • Comma splice: comma linking two ICs
  • Fragment: incomplete thought
  • Dangling modifier: nothing to modify
  • Misplaced modifier: wrong position

Verb Forms

  • Subjunctive: "If I were..."
  • Lay vs Lie: lay (transitive), lie (intransitive)
  • "Would of": WRONG — use "would have"
  • Present perfect: still relevant now
  • Past perfect: before another past event

Punctuation Quick Rules

  • Semicolon: joins 2 independent clauses
  • Colon: introduces list/explanation
  • Em dash —: emphasis/interruption
  • Oxford comma: before final item in series
  • Apostrophe: possessive + contractions only

Confused Word Pairs

  • Affect/Effect: verb/noun (usually)
  • Than/Then: comparison/time
  • Fewer/Less: countable/uncountable
  • Imply/Infer: speaker implies, listener infers
  • Complement/Compliment: complete/praise

Articles at a Glance

  • A/An: first mention, not specific
  • The: specific/known item
  • Zero article: plural/uncountable general
  • An hour: vowel SOUND, not letter
  • The Nile/Alps: rivers, mountain ranges

Common Prepositions

  • In time: years/months/seasons
  • On time: days/dates
  • At time: exact hours
  • Different from: (not "different than")
  • "Between you and me" — not "I"

Section 1: Subject-Verb Agreement Pitfalls

14 sub-topics · Compound subjects, indefinite pronouns, collective nouns & more
✓ Read ▼
1.1 Compound Subjects (and, or, nor) ▼
Rule
Subjects joined by and take a plural verb. Subjects joined by or or nor take a verb that agrees with the nearest subject.
❌ Incorrect
Tom and Sarah is going to the meeting.
Two subjects joined by "and" → plural needed
✅ Correct
Tom and Sarah are going to the meeting.
❌ Incorrect
Neither the students nor the teacher were ready.
Nearest subject "teacher" is singular
✅ Correct
Neither the students nor the teacher was ready.
For "or/nor": think of a SCALE — the verb balances with whichever subject is CLOSEST.
Exception: When both subjects are singular, use a singular verb. When both are plural, use a plural verb. The tricky part is when they're mixed!
1.2 Indefinite Pronouns ▼
Rule
Always singular: everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, no one, nobody, nothing, each, either, neither, anyone, anything.

Always plural: both, few, many, several, others.

Either singular or plural (depends on noun after "of"): some, any, none, all, most.
❌ Incorrect
Everyone in the classes have submitted their work.
✅ Correct
Everyone in the classes has submitted their work.
✅ Correct (both valid)
None of the water is contaminated. (uncountable → singular)
None of the students were absent. (countable → plural)
Remember SENSE: Someone, Everyone, Nobody, Some(one), Each — all singular. Think "one person."
⚠️ Common Pitfall
Prepositional phrases trick you: "Each of the students" — ignore "of the students," the subject is still "Each" (singular).

1.3 Collective Nouns ▼
Rule
Collective nouns (team, group, committee, jury, audience, family, class, staff) are usually singular in American English when acting as a unit. In British English, they can be plural when members act individually.
✅ American English
The team is playing well today. (acting as a unit)
✅ British English
The team are wearing different kits. (acting individually)
Key test: Are members acting as ONE unit or as INDIVIDUALS? "The jury has reached a verdict" (unit) vs. "The jury were arguing among themselves" (individuals).
1.4 Inverted Sentences — There is/are, Here is/are ▼
Rule
In "There is/are" constructions, the subject comes AFTER the verb. Find the real subject and match the verb to it.
❌ Incorrect
There is many reasons to study grammar.
✅ Correct
There are many reasons to study grammar.
Real subject = "reasons" (plural)
Flip the sentence: "Many reasons are there" → plural → use "are."
1.5 "The number of" vs "A number of" ▼
Rule
"The number of" = singular verb (focuses on the number itself)
"A number of" = plural verb (means "many" or "several")
✅ Correct
The number of students is increasing every year.
A number of students are absent today.
"THE number" = specific count (singular). "A number" = vague many (plural). THE = one, A = many.
1.6 Intervening Phrases & "Along with/As well as" ▼
Rule
Phrases like "along with," "as well as," "together with," "in addition to," and "including" do NOT change the number of the subject. They are parenthetical. Similarly, prepositional phrases between subject and verb do NOT affect agreement.
❌ Incorrect
The president, along with his advisors, were briefed.
✅ Correct
The president, along with his advisors, was briefed.
Real subject = "The president" (singular)
Bracket the intervening phrase mentally: "The president [along with his advisors] was briefed." What's left? "The president was." ✓
❌ Incorrect
The list of assignments are on the board.
✅ Correct
The list of assignments is on the board.
Subject = "list" not "assignments"
1.7 Amounts, Money, Time, Measurements as Subjects ▼
Rule
When a subject refers to a single unit of time, money, measurement, or amount, use a singular verb — even if the noun appears plural.
✅ Correct
Twenty dollars is a reasonable price.
Three hours is too long to wait.
Ten miles is a long walk.
Think of it as one amount/quantity treated as a single unit. "Twenty dollars" = one sum of money.
1.8 Titles, Company Names & Words Used as Words ▼
Rule
Titles of works, names of organizations, and words being referred to as words always take singular verbs.
✅ Correct
Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone is my favourite book.
General Motors has announced new layoffs.
"Politics" is a difficult word to define.
1.9 Gerunds as Subjects & Relative Pronouns (who/which/that) ▼
Rule
Gerunds (-ing forms used as nouns) as subjects always take singular verbs.

Relative pronouns (who, which, that) as subjects take a verb that agrees with their antecedent.
✅ Correct (Gerund)
Swimming every day improves your stamina.
Writing essays requires careful planning.
✅ Correct (Relative Pronoun)
She is one of those students who excel in exams. (who refers to "students" → plural)
He is the only student who has submitted early. (who refers to "student" → singular)
⚠️ Pitfall
"One of those who" vs "the only one who" — a classic exam trap. "One of those students who work hard" (plural, many work hard). "The only one who works hard" (singular).

Section 2: Pronoun Errors

11 sub-topics · Case, agreement, ambiguity, possessives & more
✓ Read ▼
2.1 Pronoun Case Errors — I vs Me, Who vs Whom ▼
Rule
Use subject pronouns (I, he, she, we, they, who) as the subject of a verb.
Use object pronouns (me, him, her, us, them, whom) as objects of verbs or prepositions.
❌ Incorrect
Between you and I, this is a mistake. / The letter was for Sara and I.
✅ Correct
Between you and me, this is a mistake. / The letter was for Sara and me.
After prepositions, always use object pronouns
❌ Incorrect
Who did you speak to? / To who should I address the letter?
✅ Correct
Whom did you speak to? / To whom should I address the letter?
WHO/WHOM test: Replace with he/him. If "him" sounds right → use WHOM. "I spoke to him" → "Whom did I speak to?" ✓
Remove other nouns to test: "The award was given to Sara and ___." Remove "Sara and" → "The award was given to me." ✓ Never "I."
2.2 Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement ▼
Rule
A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number (singular/plural) and gender. Singular indefinite pronoun antecedents (everyone, each, nobody) traditionally take singular pronouns.
❌ Incorrect (traditional grammar)
Everyone should bring their own pen.
✅ Correct (modern/accepted)
Everyone should bring their own pen. (singular "they" — now widely accepted)
Every student should bring his or her own pen. (formal traditional)
Modern English increasingly accepts "they/their" as singular gender-neutral pronouns for indefinite antecedents. Many style guides (APA, MLA) now endorse this.
2.3 Ambiguous & Vague Pronoun Reference ▼
Rule
Every pronoun must have a clear, specific antecedent. Avoid "this," "that," "it," and "which" that refer vaguely to a whole idea rather than a specific noun.
❌ Ambiguous
When Mary met Jane, she was nervous. (Who was nervous?)
✅ Correct
When Mary met Jane, Mary was nervous.
❌ Vague reference
He finished the project early. This impressed his boss. (What does "this" refer to?)
✅ Correct
He finished the project early. This early completion impressed his boss.
Quick test: Can you draw an arrow from the pronoun to ONE specific noun? If not, rewrite.
2.4 Reflexive Pronoun Misuse ▼
Rule
Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves) should ONLY be used for: (1) emphasis, or (2) when the subject and object are the same person.
❌ Incorrect
Please send the report to John or myself. / Myself and Tom will attend.
✅ Correct
Please send the report to John or me. / Tom and I will attend.
✅ Correct uses of reflexive
She hurt herself. (same subject/object) / I myself wrote this essay. (emphasis)
"Myself" is NOT a polite substitute for "me." If you can replace it with "me" or "I," don't use "myself."
2.5 Possessive Pronouns vs. Contractions (its/it's, their/they're, your/you're) ▼
Rule
Possessive pronouns NEVER use apostrophes: its, their, your, whose.
Apostrophe versions are CONTRACTIONS (shortened forms): it's = it is, they're = they are, you're = you are, who's = who is.
PossessiveContractionTest

❌ Incorrect
The dog wagged it's tail. / Their going to the market.
✅ Correct

ACID test — Apostrophe = Contraction Indicates Deletion. If there's no letter missing, no apostrophe needed.
2.6 Pronoun Shifts & Who vs. That for People ▼
Rule
Don't switch between "one," "you," and "they" within the same context (pronoun shift).

Use who/whom for people. Use that/which for things. That for people is acceptable informally but avoid in formal writing.
❌ Pronoun shift
If one studies hard, you will succeed and they'll pass their exams.
✅ Correct
If one studies hard, one will succeed and pass one's exams.
❌ "That" for people (formal writing)
The student that won the prize is my friend.
✅ Correct
The student who won the prize is my friend.

Section 3: Sentence Structure Errors

13 sub-topics · Run-ons, fragments, modifiers, parallelism & more
✓ Read ▼
3.1 Run-on Sentences & Comma Splices ▼
Rule
A run-on sentence joins two independent clauses with no punctuation at all. A comma splice joins them with only a comma. Both are errors.
❌ Run-on
She studied all night she passed the exam easily.
❌ Comma Splice
She studied all night, she passed the exam easily.
Four Fixes
1. Period: She studied all night. She passed easily.
2. Semicolon: She studied all night; she passed easily.
3. Conjunction: She studied all night, so she passed easily.
4. Subordinate: Because she studied all night, she passed easily.
FANBOYS (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So) can fix comma splices when added after the comma.
3.2 Sentence Fragments ▼
Rule
A sentence fragment is an incomplete sentence that is missing a subject, a verb, or both — or is a dependent clause standing alone.
❌ Fragment (missing verb)
The tall student with the red backpack.
❌ Fragment (dependent clause alone)
Although he studied for hours.
✅ Correct
The tall student with the red backpack arrived late.
Although he studied for hours, he was not fully prepared.
Test: Can it stand alone as a complete thought? Does it have a subject + verb? If not, it's a fragment.
3.3 Dangling Modifiers ▼
Rule
A dangling modifier has nothing in the sentence to logically modify — the noun it should modify is absent.
❌ Dangling
Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful.
The trees were not walking!
✅ Correct
Walking down the street, I noticed the beautiful trees.
❌ Dangling
After finishing the exam, the clock struck noon.
✅ Correct
After I finished the exam, the clock struck noon.
The subject closest to the modifier must be the DOER of the modifier's action. Ask: "Who is [modifier action]-ing?"
3.4 Misplaced & Squinting Modifiers ▼
Rule
A misplaced modifier is positioned far from the word it should modify, creating confusion. A squinting modifier could modify either the word before it or after it.
❌ Misplaced
She only eats vegetables on Tuesdays. (Does she ONLY eat? or only on Tuesdays?)
✅ Correct
She eats only vegetables on Tuesdays. / She eats vegetables only on Tuesdays.
❌ Squinting
Students who study frequently pass exams. (frequently study? or frequently pass?)
✅ Correct
Students who frequently study pass exams. / Students who study pass exams frequently.
3.5 Faulty Parallelism ▼
Rule
Items in a series, list, or comparison must be in the same grammatical form. If one element is a noun, all should be nouns. If one is an infinitive, all should be infinitives.
❌ Not parallel
She likes reading, to swim, and jogging.
✅ Parallel
She likes reading, swimming, and jogging. (all -ing gerunds)
❌ Not parallel (correlative conjunctions)
He is not only intelligent but also he works hard.
✅ Parallel
He is not only intelligent but also hardworking.
MATCH THE FORM: noun+noun, verb+verb, -ing+-ing, to+to. Think of it as a grammar symmetry check.
3.6 Faulty Predication & Illogical Comparisons ▼
Rule
Faulty predication: subject and predicate don't make logical sense together. Common with "is when," "is where," "the reason is because."

Illogical comparison: comparing unlike things.
❌ Faulty predication
The reason I failed is because I didn't study.
✅ Correct
The reason I failed is that I didn't study. / I failed because I didn't study.
❌ Illogical comparison
My essay is better than John. (comparing essay to person)
✅ Correct
My essay is better than John's (essay).
Always add "that of" or the possessive form when comparing nouns of the same type: "better than that of John" or "better than John's."

Section 4: Verb Tense & Form Errors

12 sub-topics · Tense shifts, subjunctive, irregular verbs, active/passive
✓ Read ▼
4.1 Unnecessary Tense Shifts ▼
Rule
Once you choose a tense for narrating events, stay in that tense unless there's a logical reason to shift (e.g., an action that happened before the main time frame).
❌ Incorrect
She walked into the room and sees everyone staring at her. She stopped and looks around.
✅ Correct
She walked into the room and saw everyone staring at her. She stopped and looked around.
Note: Literary present tense is acceptable when discussing literature. "In Hamlet, the prince hesitates to act." This is consistent and intentional.
4.2 Past Perfect vs. Simple Past ▼
Rule
Use past perfect (had + past participle) for an action that happened BEFORE another past action. Use simple past for a single completed action in the past.
❌ Incorrect
By the time she arrived, we ate all the food.
✅ Correct
By the time she arrived, we had eaten all the food.
"had eaten" happened before "arrived"
Timeline rule: Earlier action = past perfect. Later action = simple past. "By the time X, Y had already happened."
4.3 Present Perfect vs. Simple Past ▼
Rule
Simple past: action completed at a specific time. Present perfect: action connected to now (result still relevant, or time unspecified).
✅ Simple Past (specific time)
I finished the assignment yesterday.
✅ Present Perfect (ongoing relevance)
I have finished the assignment. (it's now done, relevant to now)
❌ Wrong with specific time word
I have visited Paris in 2018.
✅ Correct
I visited Paris in 2018.
Specific time "in 2018" → simple past
Clue words for present perfect: just, already, yet, ever, never, since, for, recently. Clue words for simple past: yesterday, last year, ago, in [year].
4.4 Subjunctive Mood (If I were / was) ▼
Rule
Use the subjunctive mood for hypothetical/unreal conditions and formal wishes. "Were" (not "was") is used for all subjects in subjunctive "if" clauses.
❌ Incorrect (formal)
If I was you, I would apologize. / I wish I was taller.
✅ Correct
If I were you, I would apologize. / I wish I were taller.
In very informal speech, "If I was" is increasingly accepted. But for academic/formal writing, always use "If I were."
IF = Imaginary → WERE. "If" + unreal scenario = subjunctive = "were" for all persons.
4.5 "Would of" and Conditional Errors ▼
Rule
"Would of," "could of," "should of," "might of" are ALWAYS WRONG. They are mishearings of "would've," "could've" etc. Always write "would have," "could have," "should have."
❌ Incorrect
I would of gone if I had known. / She could of won easily.
✅ Correct
I would have gone if I had known. / She could have won easily.
"Would OF" = impossible: "of" is a preposition, not an auxiliary verb. You HAVE done things. You never "of" done things.

4.7 Active vs. Passive Voice — When to Use Each ▼
Rule
Active voice: subject performs the action → stronger, clearer, more direct. Preferred in most writing.
Passive voice: subject receives the action → use when the doer is unknown, irrelevant, or should be de-emphasized.
❌ Weak passive (when active works better)
The report was written by Ahmed.
✅ Better active
Ahmed wrote the report.
✅ Good use of passive
The suspect was arrested. (doer irrelevant/unknown)
The experiment was conducted in a controlled environment. (scientific writing convention)
Science and law writing often prefer passive. Business and journalism prefer active. Know the context.

Section 5: Punctuation Pitfalls

Commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, dashes & more
✓ Read ▼
5.1 Comma Rules — All Major Rules ▼
RuleExample
After introductory elementAfter the exam, , we celebrated.
Before FANBOYS in compound sentenceHe studied hard, but he still failed.
Series (Oxford comma)I bought bread, milk, and eggs.
Nonrestrictive clause (extra info)My teacher, who is Egyptian, is brilliant.
AppositiveMy friend Ahmed, a doctor, helped me.
Coordinate adjectivesa tall, handsome man
⚠️ Forbidden Commas
❌ Between subject and verb: "The tall student, ran fast."
❌ Between verb and object: "She wrote, a long essay."
❌ After "that": "He said, that he was ready." (no comma after "that")
Restrictive vs Nonrestrictive
Restrictive (no commas — essential info): The student who cheated was expelled. (identifies which student)
Nonrestrictive (commas — extra info): My sister, who lives in London, called me. (sister is already identified)
5.2 Semicolons & Colons ▼
Semicolon Rules
1. Joins two independent clauses: "He studied hard; he still failed."
2. Before conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, moreover): "He worked hard; however, he failed."
3. In complex lists (when items contain commas): "We visited Cairo, Egypt; Rome, Italy; and Paris, France."
Colon Rules
1. Before a list: "Bring these items: pen, paper, and ruler."
2. Before an explanation: "There is one reason: laziness."
3. After a complete independent clause ONLY — not after "such as," "including," "for example."
❌ Wrong colon use
The ingredients include: flour, sugar, and eggs.
✅ Correct
The ingredients include flour, sugar, and eggs. (no colon needed)
You will need the following: flour, sugar, and eggs. (colon after complete clause)
5.3 Apostrophe Errors ▼
Rule
Apostrophes have TWO uses: (1) possessives and (2) contractions.
NEVER use apostrophes for plural nouns.

Signs like "Apple's for sale" or "CD's available" are WRONG. Never apostrophise simple plurals.

5.5 Quotation Marks & Punctuation Placement ▼
Rule
In American English: commas and periods go INSIDE quotation marks.
In British English: punctuation goes OUTSIDE unless part of the quoted material.
Colons and semicolons go OUTSIDE quotation marks in both systems.
✅ American
She said, "I understand." / He called it "extraordinary,"
✅ British
She said 'I understand'. / He called it 'extraordinary'.

7.1 A vs An — Vowel Sound, Not Vowel Letter ▼
Rule
Use "an" before words that BEGIN with a vowel SOUND (not just a vowel letter). Use "a" before words that begin with a consonant sound.

Test: SAY the word aloud. Does it start with a vowel SOUND? If yes → "an." Listen, don't just look.
7.2 The vs A/An — Definite vs Indefinite ▼
Rule
Use a/an: first mention, one of many, not specific.
Use the: second mention, specific/unique, both speaker and listener know which one.
Use no article (zero): plural or uncountable nouns in a general sense.
✅ Correct sequence
I saw a dog in the park. The dog was barking loudly.
First mention = a, second mention = the
✅ Zero article (general)
Cats are independent animals. / Water is essential for life.
✅ "The" for specific
The cats in my neighbourhood are noisy. / The water in this bottle is warm.

8.3 Ending a Sentence with a Preposition — Myth vs Reality ▼

The "rule" against ending sentences with prepositions is a myth based on Latin grammar. In English, ending with a preposition is perfectly acceptable, especially when the alternative is awkward.

Wordiness, passive overuse, tone, sentence variety
✓ Read ▼
9.1 Wordy Constructions & How to Fix Them ▼
Wordy✅ Concise

It is important to note that...Notably...
The fact that he was late meant...His lateness meant...

Expletive Constructions
"There is/are" and "It is" constructions often waste words. Usually, the real subject can lead the sentence instead.
❌ Weak expletive
There are three reasons why this policy failed.
✅ Stronger
This policy failed for three reasons.
9.2 Hedging Language Overuse & "I think/I believe" ▼
Rule
Avoid opening every sentence with "I think," "I believe," "In my opinion." In academic writing, these phrases weaken claims and can be redundant (it's your essay — obviously it's your opinion).
❌ Weak
I think that Shakespeare was a great writer. I believe his works explore universal themes.
✅ Stronger
Shakespeare was undeniably a great writer whose works explore universal themes.
Use hedging strategically — for genuine uncertainty or research claims: "Evidence suggests..." "It appears that..." These are legitimate and sound more academic than "I think."

Section 10: Advanced Grammar Pitfalls

Correlative conjunctions, that/which, participial phrases, absolute phrases
✓ Read ▼
10.1 Correlative Conjunctions — Parallelism Required ▼
Rule
Correlative conjunctions (either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also, both...and, whether...or) require parallel grammatical forms on both sides.
Conjunction Pair❌ Wrong✅ Correct
not only...but alsoShe not only sings but also dancingShe not only sings but also dances
either...orEither you leave or stayingEither you leave or you stay
both...andBoth tired and she was hungryShe was both tired and hungry
neither...norHe neither smiled nor he spokeHe neither smiled nor spoke
The structure AFTER the first conjunction must MIRROR the structure AFTER the second. Balance like a seesaw.
10.2 Restrictive vs Nonrestrictive Clauses — That vs Which ▼
Rule
That: restrictive clause (essential to meaning — no commas). Removing it changes meaning.
Which: nonrestrictive clause (extra information — use commas). Removing it doesn't change core meaning.
✅ Restrictive (that)
The report that was submitted late will be penalised. (identifies which report)
✅ Nonrestrictive (which)
The annual report, which is 50 pages long, was well-received. (extra info about a specific report)
THAT = essential (no commas, can't be removed). WHICH = extra (commas, removable). "THAT matters, WHICH doesn't."
10.3 Participial Phrases & Absolute Phrases ▼
Participial Phrases
A participial phrase opens with a participle (-ing or past participle). The subject of the main clause must be the one performing the action. Misuse = dangling modifier.
✅ Correct participial phrase
Having finished the exam, she submitted her paper. (she finished AND she submitted)
Frustrated by the delay, the passengers demanded refunds.
Absolute Phrases
An absolute phrase consists of a noun + participle. It modifies the entire sentence and doesn't dangle because it has its own noun.
✅ Absolute phrases
The exam finished, the students breathed a collective sigh of relief.
Her eyes closed, she concentrated on the music.
10.4 Garden-Path Sentences & Elliptical Constructions ▼
Garden-Path Sentences
Sentences that lead the reader to a wrong initial interpretation. Awareness helps you write clearer sentences.
✅ Classic examples (parsed correctly when re-read)
"The horse raced past the barn fell." → The horse [that was] raced past the barn fell. (initially confusing)
"I convinced her children are noisy." → ambiguous: convinced her THAT children are noisy, or convinced her children?
Elliptical Constructions
Omitting words understood from context. Must be grammatically parallel — the omitted words must work in the slot.
✅ Correct ellipsis
She works harder than he does. (not "than him" in formal writing)
He can play piano, and she guitar. (implied "can play")
❌ Faulty ellipsis
I like mathematics more than Ahmed. (do you like it more than Ahmed does, or more than you like Ahmed?)
10.5 Appositives — Punctuation & Placement ▼
Rule
An appositive renames or describes the noun next to it. If it's nonrestrictive (not essential), set it off with commas. If restrictive (identifies which one), no commas.
✅ Nonrestrictive appositive (commas)
My brother, Ahmed, is a doctor. (I only have one brother — the name is extra info)
✅ Restrictive appositive (no commas)
My brother Ahmed is a doctor. (I have multiple brothers — "Ahmed" identifies which one)
Error❌ Wrong✅ Correct
Correlativenot only studies but also dancingnot only studies but also dances
That vs WhichThe book which I lost was expensive.The book that I lost was expensive.
That vs WhichThe novel, that was written in 1960, is a classic.The novel, which was written in 1960, is a classic.
AppositiveMy sister, Sara is a teacher.My sister Sara is a teacher. (restrictive — multiple sisters)

"not only [adj] but also [adj]" → parallel adjectives ✓
2. "The theory ___ Einstein proposed changed physics."
